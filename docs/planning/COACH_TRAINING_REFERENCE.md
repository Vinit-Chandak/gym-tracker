# Coach training reference

Version: 2026-10-08.1

This document is the editorial source. The copy the coach actually reads is served by the
deployed app from `src/domain/coach-training-reference.ts`, once per job, so a routine's older
checkout cannot coach from different guidance. Change both together and bump the version in
both when the text changes.

## Use and limits

Apply this reference with the athlete's current goals, preferences, restrictions, equipment,
programme and logged performance. The server's action permissions and progression limits still
apply. General research does not establish an optimal rep range, load-increase cutoff or number
of exercises for every movement. Choose a workable prescription and adjust from the athlete's
response. Missing exercise-specific research does not make an exercise ineffective or justify
ignoring a feasible request. Historical personal restrictions and equipment availability from a
reference document must not overwrite current athlete records.

## Targets and training dose

- **Strength:** heavier loading generally favours dynamic maximal strength. Around 80% 1RM or
  more, often programmed as 1–6 reps, is a broad emphasis for suitable loadable compounds.
  Do not automatically apply it to every accessory, prescribe maximal attempts, or convert
  percentages into exact rep counts. Practise the movement and modality being tested.
- **Muscle growth:** roughly 5–30 reps can be effective with sufficient effort. This is a broad
  evidence range, not a mandatory progression ladder or a hard boundary. Select a narrower
  range for the exercise and training block. Do not require 15, 20 or 30 reps on every exercise
  before allowing more weight. Very light loading is not assumed equivalent in every setting.
- **Local muscular endurance:** define the task and measurement. Higher reps, historically
  above 15, are a convention rather than an optimum. Fixed-load, relative-load and timed tests
  differ. This guidance does not prescribe running or aerobic training.
- **Effort:** use a suitable target RIR for rep work. Demanding sets support hypertrophy,
  particularly with lighter loads, but failure on every set is unnecessary. No universal exact
  RIR optimum is established. Compare the effort actually logged; matching the prescribed effort is
  the ordinary outcome of a well-set target, not a sign the number was copied from it.
- **Volume:** roughly 10 weekly sets per muscle is a planning anchor, not a minimum everyone
  must reach. Some people progress with less. More volume can help with diminishing returns;
  adjust to performance, recovery and available time. Account for direct and overlapping work.
  Fractional set credit is an estimate. Do not give every overlapping muscle group its own full
  quota or increase volume simply because more exercise options exist.
- **Frequency:** regular exposure, often at least twice weekly per major muscle group, is useful
  general guidance. With weekly volume matched, hypertrophy is broadly similar across tested
  frequencies. Distribute work around recovery, adherence and skill practice.
- **Rest:** allow enough recovery to preserve intended performance. At least two minutes is a
  useful compound-lift starting point; 60–90 seconds may suit some isolation work. Pooled
  hypertrophy evidence suggests a small advantage above 60 seconds and limited average
  differences beyond about 90 seconds, but demanding sets can warrant longer rest.
- **Execution:** use controlled, repeatable, tolerable ROM and tempo. Full ROM has broad support;
  training at longer muscle lengths can be useful where appropriate. Neither painful end ranges
  nor a special slow cadence are required. Place priority strength exercises earlier. There is
  no universal compound-before-isolation rule or clear hypertrophy advantage from one order.

## Progression

- Choose the exercise's rep band, work sets, effort target and feasible load step first. Preserve
  a stable baseline long enough to assess progress instead of repeatedly moving the ceiling.
- A practical double-progression option is to build reps within that band, then add the smallest
  feasible load once the work shows it is ready: every planned work set with a rep to spare
  beyond the band's ceiling at the intended effort (its reps plus reps in reserve) in one
  session, or exactly at the ceiling in two comparable sessions. This is a coaching convention,
  not a proven optimal trigger; the older habit of always waiting for two sessions was never
  tested either. Historical guidance based on exceeding a target is a different method; do not
  combine both as hurdles.
- Reported reps in reserve are informative but tend to under-estimate by about a rep, less so
  close to failure. When an athlete's steps keep finding less in hand than was reported, ask for
  more than one rep to spare before a single session earns a step.
- Load progression and repetition progression can both work. Use the selected method and the
  app's confirmation rules; one favourable study protocol does not mandate its exact increment.
- Compare the same exercise, machine/setup, load convention, ROM, rest and intended effort.
  Incomplete work or missing effort cannot establish that all planned sets met the target.
  Confirmed symptoms or poor technique can override otherwise favourable rep counts.
- After increasing load, ask each set for what the last session predicts at the new load, then
  calibrate from the reported effort. A load-to-reps conversion is an estimate for one small
  step, not a law, and the further a set is from failure, or the more reps it has, the more of a
  guess it and the reported reserve both are. A step that misses the band clearly twice in its
  first three sessions goes back to the load before it, and is tried again after two sessions at
  the ceiling; one rep in hand short of the band is within the error of a reported RIR, and holds.
- Where the next step is so coarse it would land below the band — a 10 to 12.5 kg dumbbell is a
  quarter more — build reps at most two past the ceiling, then take the step and let it start
  below the band, at no fewer than five reps at the intended effort (a band whose bottom is five or
  fewer never starts below it), and build back up. Chasing the step into the band asked for 21
  curls on a 10–15 band: long sets end on grip, discomfort and endurance as much as on the
  muscle, and the curve that asked for them is a guess that far out. A jump too big to start at
  five even from there waits at that rep count for the reserve to grow, or for a smaller step or a
  variation; reps do not keep climbing.
- Read a set at another load than the session's against the session's load: a lighter one is
  weaker there, and a heavier one the athlete tried that fell short of the band is an attempt at
  that load, not a miss at this one. A pyramid you wrote is read step by step.
- A single session lighter than a load held just before it — a light day, a lift done off its
  usual day — is not the new baseline: progression goes on from the load held. Two lighter
  sessions running are the lighter load chosen, and a lighter load your own plan asked for is
  yours.
- A load never held in the band is not yet a baseline. A heavy single, or a jump the work had
  not shown, that cannot reach the bottom of the band even taken to failure goes back at once:
  to the last load held in the band, or to where that session puts the band. Holding it would
  ask for more reps in reserve than any set there has had.
- An estimated maximum from sets close to failure can follow a trend across load changes. It is
  never a tested maximum or a target.
- For fixed-load endurance goals, build reps or duration at that load; change resistance when
  the task calls for it. There is no universal endurance load-up cutoff.
- Less assistance means greater difficulty on assisted pull-ups/dips. Bodyweight work can
  progress through added load, reduced assistance or changed leverage. Record the setup;
  machine stack numbers and different load placements are not interchangeable. On bodyweight
  work and lunges the body is most of what is moved, so a load-to-reps conversion runs through
  the body and the added load together: a small dumbbell step on a split squat is a small step.
- Holds and carries use time/distance as appropriate. Do not convert seconds or metres to reps.
  Eccentric-only, stability and rehabilitation-style work needs task-specific progression.

## Exercise selection

Choose complementary movements that fit the athlete, with enough continuity to compare results.
Machines and free weights can both build muscle; strength gains are specific to the trained
modality. Exercise count and constant variety are not goals in themselves.

| Family                                 | Selection considerations                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Chest                                  | Stable press; optional fly/adduction work. Account for front-delt and triceps overlap.                                                                 |
| Lats and upper back                    | Vertical pulling/shoulder extension and rows can complement one another. Grip variants are alternatives, not separate requirements.                    |
| Upper traps                            | Shrugs and carries serve different tasks; carries require time/distance tracking.                                                                      |
| Front, side and rear delts             | Count pressing toward front-delt work and pulling toward rear-delts. Add direct work according to goals and total dose.                                |
| Rotator cuff, serratus and lower traps | Use the relevant control or stability task; generic heavy rep targets do not automatically apply.                                                      |
| Biceps                                 | Choose suitable elbow-flexion patterns, including requested curls when feasible. No unique rep optimum is established for each grip or shoulder angle. |
| Triceps                                | Consider overhead work and pushdown/pressing patterns while accounting for pressing volume.                                                            |
| Forearms and grip                      | Separate wrist/forearm repetitions from holds, carries and wrist-roller cycles.                                                                        |
| Quads                                  | Squat/leg-press and knee-extension patterns can complement one another. Avoid redundant volume.                                                        |
| Hamstrings                             | Hip hinges and knee curls are different tasks. Choose tolerable, repeatable setups.                                                                    |
| Glutes                                 | Squat/lunge and hip-extension patterns overlap; select useful combinations rather than every variant.                                                  |
| Hip abductors and adductors            | Direct work is goal-dependent; account for compound overlap and distinguish holds from repetitions.                                                    |
| Calves and tibialis                    | Straight- and bent-knee calf work differ; high reps are not mandatory for calves. Dorsiflexion work has no established exact optimal dose here.        |
| Core and spinal erectors               | Distinguish dynamic flexion, anti-rotation, holds and loaded hinges. Dose by the actual task and current tolerance.                                    |

## Variant-specific findings and exceptions

- Trials favouring overhead cable extensions for triceps, seated leg curls for biarticular
  hamstrings, or standing calf raises for gastrocnemius growth concern the tested positions and
  muscles. They do not establish optimal reps, prove every equipment variant equivalent, or
  require replacing a tolerable preferred exercise.
- A calf trial found similar growth with 6–10 and 20–30 reps. A short elbow-flexor trial in
  untrained adults favoured 20–30 over 8–12 reps. Neither settles a universal muscle-specific
  rep rule. The workbook provides only general guidance for Bayesian cable curls.
- A movement appearing in a multi-exercise study does not isolate its own growth effect.
  Evidence from a related grip, angle or apparatus remains an extrapolation. Muscle activation,
  soreness, pump, anecdotes and a protocol's chosen dose do not establish optimal prescriptions.
- Record unilateral reps consistently per side, step/box height, assistance and load placement.
  Keep torso angle stable in rows; changed leverage or body swing is not comparable progress.
- Nordic curls, good mornings, renegade rows, ab-wheel rollouts and lumbar-extension work need
  attention to assistance, skill, leverage and tolerance rather than an automatic generic band.
  Separate Pallof holds from press reps; track band-walk setup and gripper resistance consistently.
  A reference entry alone neither clears rehabilitation nor imposes a personal exercise ban.
- Running and lifting can coexist. Higher endurance demands can affect lower-body adaptation;
  manage combined dose, spacing and recovery according to the athlete's priorities. Easy running
  is not automatically harmful, and local muscular endurance guidance is not a running plan.

## Communication

Show the prescription clearly. Add one short, practical reason when a change, hold or request
outcome needs explanation. Give each explicit request an outcome at its scheduled assessment.
Use ordinary language; omit research IDs, versions and repeated science explanations from the
user-facing response unless specifically requested. Distinguish uncertainty internally without
adding routine disclaimers to every exercise.
